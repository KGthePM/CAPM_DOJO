## Two Ways to Think About Uncertainty

Every life cycle is a bet about what you know. **Predictive** (plan-driven, a.k.a. waterfall) bets that you can determine scope, schedule, and cost early, then execute against a baseline and control change formally. **Adaptive** (agile) bets the opposite: that requirements are genuinely unknowable up front, so you should discover them through frequent delivery and feedback.

The Agile Practice Guide — your official Domain III reference — puts it more precisely. **Iterative** means repeating cycles to *refine* the product: version one is rough, version two better, like successive coats of paint. **Incremental** means delivering the product in *usable pieces*: log-in this month, payments next month. **Agile is both at once** — short, fixed timeboxes that each produce a tested, demonstrable increment. Write that sentence down; the exam tests the difference between the two words and rewards candidates who know agile needs both.

Here is the continuum, which PMI treats as a spectrum rather than a binary: predictive → iterative → incremental → agile, with **hybrid** — a deliberate combination — available anywhere along it. Iteration *reduces risk* through repetition and feedback; increment *delivers value early* by breaking scope into slices. Agile buys you both. It does not buy you omniscience, which brings us to the honest ledger.

## The Trade-Off Ledger

Adaptive approaches shine when requirements are fluid, the customer is accessible, teams are small and empowered, and value can be delivered incrementally. Predictive approaches shine when requirements are stable and well understood, when the deliverable is safety-critical or heavily regulated, when procurement demands a fixed-price baseline, and when documentation must outlive the team.

- **Bar Exam:** when a scenario describes stable, well-understood scope with heavy regulatory oversight — think bridge construction, medical devices, government compliance — the right answer is predictive, and *not* because agile is "immature." Fit, not fashion.

The guide also gives the organizational side of the ledger (Section 6.1.2), and it maps cleanly onto the ECO's "organizational structure" objective. Characteristics that **facilitate** agility: executives willing to change; the organization willing to rethink how it reviews and assesses employees; short-term budgeting and metrics rather than only long-range goals; mature talent management; centers of competencies supporting cross-functional teams; T-shaped generalizing specialists; people dedicated to one project at a time. Characteristics that **block** agility: departmental **silos** that create dependencies instead of cross-functional teams; procurement built on short-term pricing rather than long-term competency; leaders rewarded for *local* resource efficiency instead of end-to-end **flow**; decentralized portfolios that pull people onto many projects simultaneously. When the exam asks "which environment is most/least suited to adaptive," scan for those signals.

And note which enterprise assets do the facilitating — the ECO asks for EEFs and OPAs explicitly. Facilitative **EEFs**: a culture of trust and safety, tolerance for failure as learning, collocated or well-tooled virtual workspaces ("caves and common," fishbowl windows, remote pairing), and modern collaboration technology. Facilitative **OPAs**: an organization's accumulated agile experience and lessons learned, existing team charters and working agreements, agile-friendly PMO services (training, coaching, tool templates), and escalation paths for impediment removal. Hostile versions of the same assets — siloed reporting lines, change-control boards that take six weeks, documentation mandates written for waterfall — are the EEFs/OPAs that *suppress* agility, and the servant leader's job is to reform them.

The costs are symmetrical. Predictive's weakness: feedback arrives at the end, change is expensive, and risk surfaces late — you discover the requirements were wrong precisely when fixing them costs the most. Adaptive's weakness: hard to contract fixed-price, demands constant customer participation and team discipline, produces thinner documentation and governance, and scales poorly without deliberate scaffolding.

And commit this distinction to memory, because PMI finds it delicious: predictive projects fix **scope** and let schedule/cost float through change control; agile fixes **time and cost** (the iteration is a locked box) and lets **scope flex** — scope is the variable. The backlog absorbs change; the timebox does not.

## Seventeen Signatories in a Ski Lodge

In 2001, seventeen practitioners of various "lightweight" methods met at a Utah resort and produced the **Agile Manifesto** — four values and twelve principles that are the shared DNA of every framework you will study next session. Not a methodology. A statement of values. The frameworks are what happened when people tried to live by it.

The four values, verbatim, because the exam matches language and you will recite these in December:

- **Individuals and interactions** over processes and tools
- **Working software** over comprehensive documentation
- **Customer collaboration** over contract negotiation
- **Responding to change** over following a plan

Notice what the manifesto does *not* say. It does not say documentation is worthless, or contracts evil, or plans pointless. The famous closing line of the values section acknowledges the items on the right have value — the items on the left have *more*. The exam loves this nuance: "agile teams never document anything" is a wrong answer. "Agile teams produce documentation that adds value, not documentation for its own sake" is the right one.
- *Bar Exam:* a distractor claiming the manifesto *rejects* processes, documentation, contracts, or plans is always wrong. It *prioritizes*. That single word carries points.

Each value has a working meaning. Individuals and interactions: a conversation beats an email chain; a collocated team beats a perfect toolchain. Working software: a running, tested increment is the honest measure of progress — status reports can lie, demos cannot. Customer collaboration: continuous partnership with the customer beats arguing over contract clauses when reality shifts. Responding to change: a plan is a steering wheel, not a set of handcuffs.

## Twelve Principles, Six Clusters

The twelve principles read like a management philosophy compressed by impatient engineers. Rather than twelve disconnected flashcards, group them:

**Value and delivery.** Highest priority is satisfying the customer through *early and continuous delivery* of valuable software. Deliver working software *frequently* — weeks rather than months, shorter timescales preferred. Working software is the *primary measure of progress*.

**Change and learning.** *Welcome changing requirements, even late in development* — the manifesto calls change a competitive advantage to be harnessed, not a deviation to be punished. This is the psychological gulf between the two worlds.

**People and collaboration.** Business people and developers must work together *daily*. Build projects around *motivated individuals*, give them the environment and support they need, and *trust* them to get the job done. *Self-organizing* teams produce the best architectures, requirements, and designs. The most efficient communication is *face-to-face*.

**Sustainability and quality.** Agile processes promote *sustainable development* — sponsors, developers, and users should maintain pace indefinitely; chronic overtime is a defect, not a virtue. *Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design* enhances agility.

**Simplicity.** "The art of maximizing the amount of work *not* done." Build what is needed now, not the speculative cathedral of what might someday be needed.

**Reflection.** At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then *adjusts its behavior accordingly*. That sentence is the retrospective, and you will meet it as a formal event next session.

- *Bar Exam:* know which principle a scenario is invoking. "Team demos working product every two weeks" → frequent delivery / working software as progress. "Team shortened its iteration after retrospection" → the reflection principle. Match the *behavior* to the *principle*.

## When Adaptive Fits — and When It Doesn't

The Agile Practice Guide offers **suitability filters** — tools that score a project on the factors that make agile apt or reckless.

Agile suitability runs **high** when: requirements are uncertain or rapidly evolving; the customer can be actively involved; teams are small (the guide's sweet spot is **three to nine dedicated members**); the organization can respond quickly to change; the product can be delivered and tested in increments; and early feedback is worth real money.

Suitability runs **low** when: requirements are stable and exhaustively knowable up front; the product is safety- or life-critical; regulation demands comprehensive upfront specification; contracts freeze scope; the culture is rigidly hierarchical and averse to delegation; or the customer is distant and unavailable. Forcing agile onto an unwilling organization produces cargo-cult ceremonies — standups where nobody stands for anything.

- *Bar Exam:* the examinable judgment is never "agile good, waterfall bad." It is *match the approach to context*. A candidate who answers "Scrum" to a regulated fixed-scope question is not agile; he is merely wrong.

Know how the filter actually works, because the exam does. The guide's Appendix X3 model scores **three categories** — **Culture** (buy-in to the approach, trust in the team, decision-making powers granted to the team), **Team** (size, experience levels, access to the customer/business), and **Project** (likelihood of change, criticality of the product, incremental delivery possible). Scores from 1 to 10 are plotted on a **radar chart**: values clustered near the center indicate a good fit for agile, values at the periphery indicate predictive, and the middle ring indicates **hybrid**. Two refinements the exam could ask about: the questionnaire is completed **as a group** — no single stakeholder should assess suitability alone, for the same reason no single person should estimate a project alone — and the result is a **diagnostic conversation-starter, not a verdict**: if the tool says predictive and the stakeholders reach consensus on agile, follow the stakeholders and manage the mismatch as a risk.

## Hybrid: The Grown-Up Answer

Now the part your exam actually tests hardest, because it is what real organizations do: **hybrid**. A hybrid approach deliberately combines predictive and adaptive elements to fit context — and PMBOK 7's tailoring principle explicitly licenses it.

Common patterns you should recognize on sight:

- **Predictive backbone, agile belly.** Overall project governed by a milestone plan and stage gates, while the development work inside runs in sprints.
- **Predictive wrapper, agile core.** Executive stakeholders get their baselines, earned-value reporting, and governance reviews; the delivery team works the backlog.
- **Phased split.** Predictive discovery and architecture up front (the requirements genuinely *are* knowable), then agile delivery of increments.
- **Agile with a compliance layer.** Mostly Scrum, but a regulatory workstream runs predictive because the auditor is not interested in your burndown chart.

Hybrid is not a cop-out, Mr. Gomez; it is *tailoring in action*. The wrong answers on these questions are the extremes: "convert the entire program to Scrum immediately" and "refuse to alter the methodology." The right answer adapts deliberately, within organizational policy, based on what is actually uncertain.

## Key Terms

- **Predictive life cycle** — scope, schedule, and cost fixed early; change formally controlled
- **Adaptive (agile) life cycle** — iterative plus incremental; timeboxed iterations each deliver a usable, tested increment
- **Iterative** — repeated cycles that refine the product toward correctness
- **Incremental** — delivery of the product in usable pieces, one slice at a time
- **Hybrid** — deliberate combination of predictive and adaptive elements, chosen through tailoring
- **Timebox** — fixed maximum duration for an iteration; scope flexes, time does not
- **Agile Manifesto** — 2001 statement of 4 values and 12 principles underlying all agile frameworks
- **Suitability filter** — X3 assessment scoring Culture, Team, and Project attributes on a radar chart; center = agile, periphery = predictive, middle = hybrid
- **Definable work** — low-uncertainty work with proven procedures (the predictive home turf)
- **High-uncertainty work** — exploratory work with high change, complexity, and risk (the adaptive home turf)
- **Backlog** — single ordered list of all known work; the adaptive replacement for the scope baseline
