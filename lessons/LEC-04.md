# LEC-04 — Models: The Professor's Gallery of Frameworks

*CAPM Training Dojo · Professor's Lecture Series · Session Four*

Section 4.2 of the PMBOK Guide is PMI's collection of **models** — small-scale, simplified views of reality that explain how something works and point to approaches for solving problems. You will not be asked to *build* one, Mr. Gomez — only to recognize each and match it to a situation, which is exactly how the exam tests you.

## Situational Leadership Models

These models describe how to **tailor leadership style** to the individual and the team.

**Situational Leadership® II (Blanchard).** Measures development on **competence** (ability, knowledge, skill) and **commitment** (confidence and motivation). As these evolve, style evolves: **directing → coaching → supporting → delegating**. Gloss: you do not lecture a sophomore, and you do not hover over a PhD candidate.
- *Bar Exam:* match style to the *development level*, not the leader's mood.

**OSCAR (Whittleworth and Gilbert).** A coaching model for individuals with a development action plan: **Outcome** (long-term goals, each session's desired result), **Situation** (current skills and knowledge, and why), **Choices/consequences** (all avenues, so the person chooses), **Actions** (specific, attainable targets in a time frame), **Review** (regular meetings to stay on track). Gloss: a coaching conversation with a spine.
- *Bar Exam:* development plan plus check-ins → OSCAR; style matching → SLII.

## Communication Models

These models show how sender and receiver **frames of reference** shape effectiveness, how the **medium** influences the message, and where user expectations disconnect from reality.

**Cross-Cultural Communication (Browaeys and Price).** The message is shaped by the sender's knowledge, experience, language, thinking, style, stereotypes, and relationship to the receiver — and the receiver interprets through the same filters. Gloss: two people, two funhouse mirrors, one message.
- *Bar Exam:* miscommunication traces to filters on *both ends*, never one "difficult" party.

**Channel Effectiveness (Cockburn; richness per Daft and Lengel).** Channels are plotted on **effectiveness and richness**; richness is a medium's ability to handle multiple cues simultaneously, facilitate rapid feedback, establish personal focus, and use natural language. Complex or personal information demands **rich channels** — face-to-face; simple facts can travel **lean channels** — a note or text. Gloss: you do not break up by telegram.
- *Bar Exam:* bad news or complexity gets the richest channel.

**Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation (Norman).** The **gulf of execution** is the gap between a user's intention and what the item allows — you press "park" and the car does not park. The **gulf of evaluation** is the degree to which the item helps the user figure out how to interpret and interact with it.
- *Bar Exam:* execution = "it won't let me do what I meant"; evaluation = "I can't tell what it's doing." They will swap them on you.

## Motivation Models

People are motivated by *different things*; understanding this lets you tailor rewards to the individual.

**Hygiene and Motivational Factors (Herzberg).** **Motivational factors** relate to the *content of the work* — achievement, growth, advancement; sufficient ones produce satisfaction. **Hygiene factors** relate to the *context* — policies, salary, environment; insufficient hygiene causes dissatisfaction, but sufficient hygiene *never creates satisfaction*. Gloss: salary is a hot shower — pleasant, expected, and incapable of making you love your job.
- *Bar Exam:* "we raised salaries, why is morale flat?" — pay is hygiene, not motivator.

**Intrinsic versus Extrinsic Motivation (Pink).** Extrinsic rewards motivate only up to fair pay; for challenging work, intrinsic motivators last longer: **autonomy** (directing one's own life — how, where, when), **mastery** (improving and excelling), **purpose** (making a difference through the project vision).
- *Bar Exam:* know autonomy/mastery/purpose by name — a favorite matching question.

**Theory of Needs (McClelland).** People are driven by **achievement** (challenging but reasonable goals), **power** (organizing, motivating, leading), and **affiliation** (acceptance and belonging); relative strength depends on experience and culture. Gloss: some run races, some run for office, some just want on the committee.
- *Bar Exam:* challenge-seeker → achievement; aspiring leader → power; team-joiner → affiliation.

**Theory X, Y, and Z (McGregor; extended).** **Theory X** assumes people work solely for income — inviting top-down management, seen in production. **Theory Y** assumes intrinsic motivation — inviting a coaching style that encourages creativity, seen in knowledge workers. **Theory Z** has two flavors: Maslow's transcendent view (self-realization, values, higher calling) and Ouchi's (job-for-life focus on employee and family well-being). Gloss: X bosses watch the clock, Y bosses coach, Z bosses ask about your soul.
- *Bar Exam:* top-down production → X; coaching knowledge workers → Y; lifetime employment → Ouchi's Z.

## Change Models

Projects change systems, behaviors, and cultures — so we manage the transition from current to future state. Five models; learn which is which.

**Managing Change in Organizations (PMI Practice Guide).** An *iterative* model of five elements linked by feedback loops: **formulate change** (build the rationale — why change, why the future state is better), **plan change** (prepare people), **implement change** (demonstrate capabilities, check impact, adapt), **manage transition** (address needs that surface in the future state), **sustain change** (new capabilities continue, old behaviors cease).
- *Bar Exam:* the keyword is *iterative* with feedback loops — not a one-way march.

**ADKAR® (Hiatt).** Five *sequential* steps *individuals* undergo: **Awareness** (why change is necessary), **Desire** (to support it), **Knowledge** (how to change — new processes and roles, via training), **Ability** (hands-on practice and expertise), **Reinforcement** (rewards, recognition, feedback, measurement). Gloss: you cannot want what you do not know about, and you cannot do what you have not practiced.
- *Bar Exam:* individual-level and sequential; "aware but lacks skills" → Knowledge or Ability.

**The 8-Step Process for Leading Change (Kotter).** A *top-down* approach promoted down through management: **create urgency; form a powerful coalition** (influential leaders, not necessarily hierarchy); **create a vision for change; communicate the vision; remove obstacles; create short-term wins; build on the change; anchor the changes in corporate culture**. Gloss: light the fire at the top, then feed it until the culture itself burns.
- *Bar Exam:* "short-term wins" and "anchor in culture" are Kotter's signature phrases.

**Virginia Satir Change Model.** How people *experience and cope* with change: **late status quo**, the **foreign element** (change arrives; resistance, performance dips), **chaos** (performance lowest; unpredictable feelings, though chaos sparks creativity), the **transforming idea** (performance climbs), **practice and integration** (trial and error; performance often ends *higher* than before), the **new status quo**. Gloss: the dip before the rise.
- *Bar Exam:* lowest performance = chaos; performance often exceeds the old baseline after practice and integration.

**Transition Model (Bridges).** Distinguishes **change** (situational; happens whether or not people come along) from **transition** (the *psychological* acceptance of it). Three stages: **ending, losing, letting go** (fear, anger, denial); the **neutral zone** (frustration or creativity; productivity may drop); the **new beginning** (acceptance, even embrace). Gloss: the org chart changed on Monday; the people catch up by spring.
- *Bar Exam:* Bridges owns *transition* and the *neutral zone* — see "neutral zone," do not reach for Satir.

## Complexity Models

Projects live in ambiguity; two frameworks teach you how to decide.

**Cynefin Framework (Snowden).** Five contexts. **Obvious** cause-and-effect: apply **best practices**. **Complicated** (known unknowns, a range of correct answers): apply **good practices**. **Complex** (unknown unknowns): **probe, sense, respond** with **emergent practices**, repeating the cycle. **Chaotic** (too much confusion to wait): **act to stabilize, then sense, then respond**. **Disordered**: break it into parts that fit the other four. Gloss: clear rules for clear days, experiments for fog, and in a fire you grab the extinguisher *first*.
- *Bar Exam:* memorize the pairings — obvious/best, complicated/good, complex/probe-sense-respond, chaotic/act-sense-respond.

**Stacey Matrix (Stacey).** Plotted on two dimensions: relative **uncertainty of requirements** and of the **technology**; the combination classifies the project as **simple, complicated, complex, or chaotic**, informing tailoring. Gloss: two dials — fuzzy wants and fuzzy hows.
- *Bar Exam:* Stacey = requirements uncertainty × technology uncertainty. Two axes, no more.

## Project Team Development Models

Teams move through stages; knowing the stage tells the manager how to help.

**Tuckman Ladder (Tuckman).** **Forming** — the team first meets; names, positions, skill sets, often at the kickoff. **Storming** — members jockey for position; personalities emerge; conflict over how to work together. **Norming** — the team functions as a collective; issues resolve quickly. **Performing** — operational efficiency; synergy and high-quality work. **Adjourning** — the work completes and the team disperses. Two caveats the exam adores: teams can move **back and forth**, and **not all teams reach performing — or even norming**. Gloss: they meet, squabble, settle, shine, scatter — with relapses.
- *Bar Exam:* "personalities clashing over roles" → storming; "teams always progress" is false, always.

**Drexler/Sibbet Team Performance Model.** Seven steps; 1–4 create the team, 5–7 sustain performance: **Orientation** (why — purpose and mission), **trust building** (who — members' skills, influential stakeholders), **goal clarification** (what — requirements, acceptance criteria), **commitment** (how — plans, schedules, budgets), **implementation** (produce deliverables), **high performance** (synergy, minimal oversight), **renewal** (working through changes, possibly resetting to an earlier stage). Gloss: why, who, what, how — then do it brilliantly, then renegotiate.
- *Bar Exam:* why → orientation, who → trust building, what → goal clarification, how → commitment.

## Other Models

A final room of assorted frameworks.

**Conflict Model (Thomas and Kilmann).** Conflict can be healthy — greater trust, deeper commitment — or unhealthy if addressed badly. Six approaches, keyed to relative power and desire to maintain the relationship: **confronting/problem solving** (treat conflict as a problem to solve), **collaborating** (multiple views toward consensus; needs trust and time), **compromising** (give and take between equal powers), **smoothing/accommodating** (harmony over the disagreement; used with power differences, as with a sponsor), **forcing** (imposing will when there is no time — health-and-safety), **withdrawal/avoiding** (the problem may resolve itself, or a no-win scenario like a regulatory requirement). Gloss: six doors out of a fight; pick the one the situation selects.
- *Bar Exam:* health-and-safety emergency → forcing; sponsor disagreement → smoothing; equal power → compromising.

**Negotiation (Covey's "Think Win-Win").** Outcomes: **win-win** (optimal; each satisfied), **win-lose/lose-win** (competition, or a martyr), **lose-lose** (competition overwhelms collaboration; everyone worse off). Win-win rests on **character** (maturity, integrity, enough value for everybody), **trust** (agreements, accountability), and **approach** (the other's view; options together). Gloss: the only negotiation worth winning is the one where nobody loses.
- *Bar Exam:* win-win = character, trust, approach — the three-part list is testable.

**Planning (Boehm).** Weighs planning effort (reducing uncertainty, oversights, rework) against the cost of *overplanning* (slower return, lost market share, changed circumstances). The goal is the **sweet spot** — the optimum amount of planning, different for every project; beyond it, more planning is *counterproductive*. Gloss: plan until planning stops paying for itself; then stop, Professor, I beg you.
- *Bar Exam:* "an ideal amount of planning exists per project" — that is Boehm.

**Process Groups.** Five logical groupings: **Initiating** (define and authorize), **Planning** (establish scope, refine objectives), **Executing** (complete the planned work), **Monitoring and Controlling** (track, review, regulate, initiate changes), **Closing** (formally complete a project, phase, or contract). Crucial: Process Groups are **not project phases**; they interact within each phase, may iterate, and are **independent of delivery approach, application area, and industry**. Gloss: five drawers in one cabinet every project opens repeatedly — not five rooms you visit once.
- *Bar Exam:* "Process Groups = phases" is false, every time it appears.

**Salience Model (Mitchell, Agle, Wood).** A stakeholder model; *salience* means prominent, noticeable, or perceived as important. Stakeholders are identified on **power** (ability to influence), **legitimacy** (of the relationship with the project), and **urgency** (of the stakeholder's claim). Gloss: which stakeholder gets your Friday afternoon — the powerful, the proper, or the one whose hair is on fire.
- *Bar Exam:* salience = power + legitimacy + urgency — a stakeholder prioritization model.

## Key Terms

- **Model** — a small-scale, simplified view of reality that explains how something works and points to solution approaches
- **Situational Leadership II** — Blanchard's model matching leadership style (directing, coaching, supporting, delegating) to team member competence and commitment
- **OSCAR** — coaching model: Outcome, Situation, Choices/consequences, Actions, Review
- **Media richness** — a channel's capacity to convey learning via multiple cues, rapid feedback, personal focus, and natural language
- **Hygiene factors** — Herzberg's job-context conditions (salary, policy, environment) whose absence dissatisfies but whose presence never satisfies
- **Autonomy, mastery, purpose** — Pink's three intrinsic motivators that outlast extrinsic rewards once pay is fair
- **ADKAR** — Hiatt's sequential individual change steps: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement
- **Cynefin Framework** — Snowden's five decision contexts (obvious, complicated, complex, chaotic, disordered) each with its own response pattern
- **Tuckman Ladder** — forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning — traversed nonlinearly; not all teams reach performing
- **Transition vs. change** — Bridges: change is situational; transition is the psychological acceptance of it
- **Process Groups** — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring and Controlling, Closing — organizing structure, not project phases
- **Salience Model** — prioritizing stakeholders by power, legitimacy, and urgency

## Self-Check Questions

1. A team member is highly skilled but discouraged after two failed sprints. Using Situational Leadership II, which variables are at play, and toward which style should the leader move — and why not further down the ladder?
2. Your organization doubles everyone's salary, yet the best engineers remain disengaged. Which motivation model explains this, and which three intrinsic motivators should you appeal to instead?
3. A team has worked together for three months; arguments over roles have faded, disagreements resolve quickly, and throughput is climbing but not yet synergistic. Name the Tuckman stage, the next stage, and the two caveats PMI attaches to this model.

Written answers at our next session, Mr. Gomez. Notice how often "it depends on the situation" was today's correct answer — the models simply teach you *what it depends on*. That is scholarship, not indecision.

## Addendum — Leadership vs. Management, the PM's Six Hats, and EQ

Three topics the exam grafts onto today's gallery, Mr. Gomez, so we graft them here.

**Leadership versus management.** The distinction is clean and testable. **Management** — planning, budgeting, organizing, staffing, controlling — exists to produce **predictability and order**. **Leadership** — establishing vision, aligning people, motivating and inspiring — exists to produce **change**. The project manager must practice *both*, and per Principle 6, leadership is not the private property of the person holding the title: any team member may demonstrate it.

**The project manager's six hats.** The ECO lists six roles by name, and PMI likes to hand you a scenario and ask which hat is on your head:

- **Initiator** — champions the work: proposes the project or improvement, frames the problem, and gets things moving rather than waiting to be told.
- **Negotiator** — negotiates for resources, scope trade-offs, and agreements — with functional managers, vendors, and stakeholders — aiming for the win-win.
- **Listener** — hears the team and stakeholders *fully* before acting; absorbs concerns and nonverbal signals without composing a rebuttal mid-sentence.
- **Coach** — develops team members' capability — OSCAR and Situational Leadership II from today's gallery are the coach's instruments.
- **Working member** — rolls up the sleeves and does project work directly; routine on small or adaptive teams, and nothing to sneer at in a professor either.
- **Facilitator** — makes collaboration *easy*: coordinates meetings, removes impediments, and contributes unbiased support toward group objectives without steering to a personal preference.

**Emotional intelligence (EQ).** The ability to **recognize one's own emotions and those of others, and to use that information to guide thinking and behavior**. The convergent models give four areas — learn them by name:

- **Self-awareness** — realistic self-assessment: one's own emotions, goals, motivations, strengths, and weaknesses.
- **Self-management** (self-regulation) — controlling and redirecting disruptive impulses; thinking before acting, suspending snap judgment.
- **Social awareness** — empathy: considering others' feelings, reading nonverbal cues and body language.
- **Social skill** — the culmination of the other three: managing groups, building networks, finding common ground, building rapport.

Some models add a fifth area, **motivation** — understanding what drives and inspires people. Why the exam cares: EQ is described as the *basis of all forms of leadership*, and the self-directed areas keep a PM calm and productive in difficult circumstances while the social areas build the bonds with team and stakeholders. A PM who cannot read the room is merely furniture in it.
