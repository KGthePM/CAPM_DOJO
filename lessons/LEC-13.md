## The PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

The Code rests on **four values**, each carrying **aspirational standards** (what you strive to) and **mandatory standards** (what you must do). Learn the values cold; the exam hands you a predicament and expects you to name the value in play.

**Responsibility.** Ownership of your decisions and their consequences, and fidelity to the rules. Mandatory standards include: you must **report unethical or illegal conduct** to appropriate management or, if needed, to bodies affected by the deception; you must **bring violations to the attention of the appropriate body for resolution**; you only accept assignments **consistent with your background, experience, skills, and qualifications**; you must **comply with laws, regulations, and organizational policies** — and when you develop them, comply with them too, and **inform yourself and uphold** them.

- *Scenario fingerprints:* discovering falsified test results and deciding whether to speak up (Responsibility — report it); quietly taking on work you are not qualified to lead (violation — Responsibility).

**Respect.** Regard for the dignity, rights, and contributions of others. You **inform yourself about the norms and customs of others** before working among them and **avoid engaging in activities that violate* their customs, within the bounds of law; you **listen to others' points of view**, even when you disagree; you **approach conflict directly** using facts and reason rather than coercion; you do not **abuse your position or professional standing** to influence decisions for personal benefit; you do not act **abusively** toward anyone.

- *Scenario fingerprints:* a PM bullies a vendor's analyst or trades on their title to force a decision (violation — Respect); a PM researches cultural norms before an international site visit (Respect, met).

**Fairness.** Decisions free from favoritism and self-interest, and a **duty to disclose conflicts of interest**. Mandatory standards: you **fully disclose** any real or potential conflicts of interest and let **stakeholders decide** how to proceed; when conflicts render your objectivity compromised, you **refrain from decision making**; you do not **discriminate** based on race, gender, age, religion, or any other ground; you do not **grant or accept** inappropriate payments, gifts, or favors for professional advantage; you do not hire or fire, reward or punish, based on personal considerations.

- *Scenario fingerprints:* the classic — a PM has a financial stake in a bidder and sits on the selection panel (disclose and recuse — Fairness); a PM accepts an lavish gift from a vendor mid-procurement (violation — Fairness).

**Honesty.** Truth in communications and in the work itself. You must not **engage in or condone deception**; you must not make **misleading or false statements** or **half-truths**, or provide **incomplete information** in a way that misleads; you do not **misrepresent** your qualifications or those of your team.

- *Scenario fingerprints:* reporting a slipping schedule optimistically to keep the sponsor calm (violation — Honesty); a vendor's statement "technically true but designed to mislead" (Honesty, violated — half-truths are lies wearing fine clothes).

The professor's decision procedure for any ethics scenario: **(1) name the facts, (2) name the value in tension, (3) choose the answer that discloses, reports, complies, and treats people with dignity.** The correct exam answer is *never* the quiet one, the convenient one, or the one that protects your career. It is the one that protects everyone else's.

- *Bar Exam:* when two values seem to compete, the tested values are complementary, not competing — reread the stem; usually only one value is actually in tension, and the others are decoration.

## Projects as Vehicles for Change

The ECO asks you to explain how a project can be a vehicle for change, and the answer is the spine of Principle 12 plus the change models from Session Four. A project moves an organization **from a current state to a desired future state** — that is its entire reason for existing. The deliverables are the mechanism; the *change* is the point. Three exam-grade points:

- **The transition is the product.** Deliverables that nobody adopts produce no outcomes, no benefits, no value. Enabling change therefore requires structures, strategies, and *stakeholder engagement and motivation* — not merely shipping the widget.
- **Resistance is a design input, not a surprise.** People resist change — fear of loss, disruption of habit, threatened status. The PM plans for adoption the way they plan for weather: assuming it will happen and bringing a coat.
- **Change fatigue is real.** Too much change too fast exhausts the organization; too little leaves value unrealized. The skill is pacing — sequencing change so the organization can absorb it, and using short-term wins (Kotter's contribution) to build momentum for the rest.

## Project Initiation and Benefit Planning

Initiation is where the project earns the right to exist — and where its benefits are first promised. The working sequence:

- The **business case** argues *whether and why* to undertake the project — the documented economic and non-economic benefits, the options considered, the recommendation. It is the sponsor's instrument, and the project is chartered *because of* it, not the reverse.
- The **project charter** — issued by the sponsor — **formally authorizes** the project, names the PM and their authority, and records the high-level objectives, milestones, and assumptions. Before the charter, you have an idea; after it, you have a project.
- **Benefits identification and tracking** live alongside: the intended benefits named in the business case must be **measurable** and assigned ownership, because benefits realization does not end at closure — recall from Session Six that the *sponsor* monitors outcomes after closure to confirm the intended benefits arrive. An organization serious about this maintains a **benefits management plan** (the how-and-when of benefit measurement) and reviews benefit trends over time.
- Initiation is also where **stakeholders are first identified** (principle: proactive and early) and where the high-level scope, constraints, and assumptions are recorded in the charter and the assumption log.

*Bar Exam:* the exam adores asking "what comes first?" — business case *justifies*, charter *authorizes*, and benefits are tracked *after* delivery by the sponsor's watch. Never let the charter arrive before the business case in a sequence question.

## Following Planned Strategies and Frameworks

A strategy or framework — a communications plan, a risk management plan, a governance framework — is a promise about behavior. The exam tests whether you keep it. The correct response to a planned strategy is to **execute it as planned and keep it current**:

- **Follow the plan's cadence and channels.** If the communications plan promises a weekly status report to the steering committee, the correct action when information emerges is to route it through the plan — not to improvise a hallway campaign.
- **When reality diverges, change the plan — do not abandon it.** Frameworks include the means of their own amendment: risk triggers fire the planned response; the change control plan governs scope changes; deviations are handled by revising the plan through its own procedures, not by quiet improvisation.
- **Recognize a framework breach.** A scenario where a stakeholder is blindsided by news is really a scenario about a communications plan not followed. The remedy is to follow the plan — and if the plan no longer suits, revise it deliberately.

## Closure and Transitions

Closure is a *process*, not a shrug of relief, and the ECO tests its mechanics. A proper closure includes:

- **Verification and acceptance** — deliverables are validated against acceptance criteria and **formally accepted by the sponsor or designated approver**; the closeout meeting is where final acceptance is confirmed.
- **Transition to operations** — deliverables are **handed over to the group that will operate and support them**, with the training, documentation, and support arrangements the operators need. A deliverable handed over without a transition plan is an abandoned child, not a finished project.
- **Financial and administrative close** — contracts closed, procurement settled, records archived per governance requirements.
- **Lessons learned and knowledge capture** — the retrospective or lessons-learned session held while memories are fresh, results stored where future projects can actually find them.
- **Benefits hand-off** — agreement on who (classically, the **sponsor** and the receiving operations owners) will monitor whether the promised benefits materialize *after* the team disperses.
- **Resource release** — team members reassigned with notice and dignity; on adaptive teams with stable product ownership, the team may simply continue as the product's stewards.

*Bar Exam:* the recurring trap is the project declared "done" when the code ships. Not so: **no formal acceptance and transition, no closure.** And the second trap: asking who verifies benefits after closure — the answer is the sponsor's function, not the departed PM's.

## Meetings and Group Techniques: Purpose and Evaluation

**Evaluating meeting effectiveness.** A meeting is effective when it achieves its **objective** — a decision made, information shared, problem solved — at a cost proportionate to its outcome. The evaluable dimensions: a **stated purpose and agenda** known in advance; the **right people** present (those with authority to decide and those with knowledge to inform); **time-boxed** discussion that stays on the topic; **documented decisions and action items with owners and due dates**; and a **cost check** — attendees × duration × rate — against the value produced. A meeting that ends without decisions or owners was not a meeting; it was a séance.

- *Scenario fingerprints:* the recurring status meeting with no agenda and no decisions — the correct answer proposes purpose, agenda, and outcomes, not "cancel all meetings" (the exam dislikes zealots).

**Focus groups.** A moderator-led session with **prequalified stakeholders and subject matter experts** interacting to **elicit expectations and attitudes** about a proposed product, service, or result — the *interaction* is the point; participants build on one another's reactions in a way a survey cannot capture. Use when you need qualitative depth: perceptions, expectations, concerns.

**Brainstorming.** A rapid, **non-judgmental** group idea-generation technique: collect ideas on a problem without critique during generation, then evaluate afterward. The quantity-first, judgment-later rule is what distinguishes it from ordinary argument, and the exam's pet error is a scenario where a facilitator critiques ideas mid-stream — that is brainstorming *done wrong*.

**Standup meetings.** The adaptive fixture: short, daily, time-boxed; each member covers **yesterday, today, obstacles**; blockers raised are removed *after* the standup by the right owner, not litigated in the meeting itself.

**Other named techniques.** **Interviews** (one-on-one, direct and deep), **surveys** (broad reach, shallow interaction), **workshops** (structured working sessions producing an agreed output, like requirements), and the **lessons-learned session** (experience captured for successors). When the exam hands you a need — depth, breadth, consensus, interaction — match the instrument: perceptions-with-interaction → focus group; breadth → survey; consensus artifact → workshop; daily synchronization → standup.

## Key Terms

- **Code of Ethics values** — Responsibility, Respect, Fairness, Honesty; each with aspirational and mandatory standards
- **Conflict of interest** — a real or apparent situation impairing or appearing to impair one's judgment; duty to disclose and recuse (Fairness)
- **Half-truth** — technically accurate statement arranged to mislead; a violation of Honesty
- **Change vehicle** — the project's purpose of moving the organization from current state to desired future state
- **Business case → charter** — justification precedes authorization; benefits are tracked after delivery
- **Benefits realization** — verifying promised business value materializes; sponsor's post-closure watch
- **Transition to operations** — handover of deliverables, training, and support to the operating group
- **Formal acceptance** — sponsor or designated approver confirms deliverables meet acceptance criteria; prerequisite to closure
- **Effective meeting** — clear purpose, right participants, time-boxed agenda, documented decisions and owners
- **Focus group** — moderated, interactive session with prequalified participants to elicit expectations and attitudes
- **Brainstorming** — non-judgmental group idea generation; evaluation deferred until after generation
