# LEC-02 — The 8 Performance Domains

*CAPM Training Dojo · Professor's Lecture Series · Session Two*

Mr. Gomez. Last time, principles. Today, terrain.

You will recall that the 7th Edition abandoned the old Knowledge Areas — those ten tidy silos with their ITTOs — in favor of a **systems view**. The replacement is eight **project performance domains**: groups of related activities critical for the effective delivery of project outcomes. Collectively, they form *an integrated, interactive, interdependent system of management capabilities working in unison*. That sentence is your framing device, so let me say it the way I say it to every cohort:

**The domains are not chapters of a rulebook. They are organs of one body.** They run *concurrently* — not sequentially — throughout the project, from kickoff to closure, regardless of how the project delivers value. They overlap. They interconnect. When one is disturbed, the others feel it. A student who studies them as eight separate memorized lists will pass exactly nothing.

And note the hierarchy: the **principles guide behavior; the domains are where that behavior is demonstrated**. Principles sit above the domains like a conductor above the orchestra. PMI is quite firm about this distinction, and so am I.

One more framing point before we descend into the particulars: the domains are presented *without weighting or prescribed order*, and the activities within each are shaped by **context** — organization, project, deliverables, team, stakeholders. Success is judged by **outcomes achieved**, not by adherence to processes or production of artifacts. A binder full of beautiful plans that nobody used earns you nothing here. Onward.

## 1. Stakeholder Performance Domain

Projects are performed *by* people and *for* people. This domain covers identifying stakeholders, understanding their interests and influence, and engaging them to foster productive working relationships — so that stakeholders remain supportive (or at least not sabotaging) and are satisfied with engagement and outcomes. Remember: stakeholders come and go across the life cycle, and their power and interest *shift*. Engagement is not a one-time act; it is a maintained condition, like a garden or a reputation.

- *How the exam probes it:* scenario questions about a previously supportive stakeholder turned hostile mid-project, or a forgotten stakeholder group. The correct answers emphasize *proactive identification*, *ongoing* two-way engagement, and adapting to changing influence — never "send an updated email" or "escalate immediately."

## 2. Team Performance Domain

The people doing the work, and the culture they work in. This domain concerns shared ownership of objectives, a team culture of high performance, and the interpersonal skills that make it go: **emotional intelligence** (recognizing one's own emotions and others', and using that to guide behavior), decision making, conflict resolution, and motivation — both **intrinsic** (achievement, autonomy, purpose) and extrinsic. A shared team identity, a team charter or agreements establishing norms, psychological safety to take interpersonal risk. The outcome being checked: a team that trusts, adapts, and delivers — not merely a team that is "fully allocated."

- *How the exam probes it:* questions about motivating a disengaged team member (identify their *dominant motivator*), or resolving conflict (address it early, directly, collaboratively — not by pretending it isn't happening). Tailor motivation to the individual; one incentive does not fit all.

## 3. Development Approach and Life Cycle Performance Domain

Here the team decides *how* the work will unfold. **Predictive** (requirements well understood up front, sequential phases), **adaptive** (iterating in short cycles with customer feedback at each turn), or **hybrid** (a deliberate combination — say, adaptive software development inside a predictive construction shell). The choice is driven by requirements uncertainty, technology maturity, delivery cadence, and stakeholder needs. Also in scope: **life cycle phases** (start, organize, carry out, close), and the distinction between a project *life cycle* and the *development approach* chosen within it. Cadence options include a single delivery, multiple periodic deliveries, or frequent continuous delivery.

- *How the exam probes it:* "requirements are stable and well-defined" → predictive. "High uncertainty, evolving requirements, frequent customer feedback" → adaptive. Beware the trap of assuming agile is always correct; PMI is scrupulously neutral and rewards matching approach to context.

## 4. Planning Performance Domain

Planning is about making the work *proceed in an organized, coordinated, deliberate manner* — estimating, scheduling, and organizing scope, budget, resources, and change control *appropriately for the project*. The critical word is appropriately: planning is **ongoing and evolving**, not a one-time initiation ritual. Expect emphasis on rolling-wave planning (elaborate near-term work in detail, defer the distant), developing the project's measures of success, and preparation for uncertainty. In adaptive environments, planning artifacts are lightweight and continuously revised rather than monolithic and baselined.

- *How the exam probes it:* "the team detailed only the next two months and left later phases coarse-grained" — that is correct rolling-wave behavior, not negligence. Also distinguishing initial planning from *ongoing* replanning as new information emerges.

## 5. Project Work Performance Domain

The domain of execution: managing the *processes* of the project, balancing competing constraints (scope, schedule, cost, resources, quality), procuring what's needed, developing the team, managing changes, and continuously improving how work gets done. It is where plans meet the grinding wheel of reality. Includes establishing efficient project processes, coordinating resources, and adapting as circumstances shift.

- *How the exam probes it:* process improvement questions (learn from experience, refine processes), procurement-versus-build decisions, and prioritization when constraints collide — the correct framing is *balance among competing demands*, not fanatical devotion to any one constraint.

## 6. Delivery Performance Domain

The domain of scope and quality — delivering what was promised. Meeting requirements, delivering **value**, and achieving the outcomes the project was chartered for. Central concepts: the difference between **deliverables** (the outputs) and **outcomes** (the changes they enable), and **cost of change** — in predictive approaches, the cost of change rises steeply over time; adaptive approaches are designed to keep it flatter by gathering feedback early. **Cost of quality** also lives nearby: prevention costs, appraisal costs, and the internal/external failure costs of getting it wrong. Quality, per the principles, is *built in* — validated against acceptance criteria and stakeholder expectations.

- *How the exam probes it:* questions linking deliverables → outcomes → value, and questions on why early feedback matters (it capitalizes on the low-cost-to-change window). Inspect-quality-in at the end is always the wrong answer — always.

## 7. Measurement Performance Domain

If you cannot measure it, you are not managing it — you are narrating it. This domain covers assessing performance, comparing actuals against the plan, and using data for decision making. Key tools: **key performance indicators** (leading indicators, which predict; lagging indicators, which report what already happened — a balanced measurement diet uses both), and the **earned value** family: planned value, earned value, actual cost, and derived indices like **SPI** (schedule performance) and **CPI** (cost performance), plus forecasts such as estimate to complete. Measurement exists to *enable action and forecast outcomes* — not to decorate a status report.

- *How the exam probes it:* know SPI and CPI cold and what "greater than 1" versus "less than 1" means in each; know the leading-versus-lagging distinction; and reject any answer that collects metrics but never acts on them.

## 8. Uncertainty Performance Domain

The final domain is the weather system around everything else. Uncertainty is the general condition; within it live specific characters: **ambiguity** (multiple plausible meanings, emerging states), **complexity** (systems-based, reframeable, or process-based), **volatility** (rapid, unpredictable change in materials, skills, cost, schedule), and **risk** proper (individual uncertain events) plus **overall project risk**. Responses are proportionate to the threat or opportunity: for volatility, *alternatives analysis* and *reserves*; for ambiguity, gather more information and establish governing rules; for complexity, decouple, simulate, iterate. The general posture is not "eliminate uncertainty" — impossible — but navigate it, with buffers and learning built in.

- *How the exam probes it:* questions asking which condition a scenario describes (a price spike is *volatility*; an emerging unforeseeable risk is *ambiguity*), and whether the response matches the condition — you do not answer a volatile supplier market with a team-building exercise, Mr. Gomez.

## The Interconnected Whole

Let me close with the picture I want burned into your cortex. A stakeholder concern (Domain 1) reshapes requirements, which alters the development approach (3), which forces replanning (4) and new project work (5), changing what gets delivered (6), which is captured in the measures (7), all under shifting uncertainty (8), executed by the team you built along the way (2). Disturb one organ, the body responds. PMI's own text says the team responds to changes "with the whole system in mind" — and the exam tests precisely that instinct for second-order effects.

Study accordingly. Not eight silos. One system, eight facets.

## Key Terms

- **Performance domain** — group of related activities critical for effective delivery of project outcomes
- **Systems view** — domains as interactive, interrelated, interdependent capabilities operating concurrently
- **Predictive / adaptive / hybrid** — development approaches matched to requirements certainty and feedback cadence
- **Rolling-wave planning** — progressively elaborating near-term detail while leaving distant work coarse
- **Leading vs. lagging indicators** — predictive measures vs. after-the-fact measures
- **SPI / CPI** — earned value indices for schedule and cost efficiency (values >1 favorable)
- **Cost of change** — rising cost of late correction; the economic argument for early feedback
- **Uncertainty family** — ambiguity, complexity, volatility, risk; navigated, not eliminated

## Self-Check Questions

1. Why does PMI insist the performance domains are evaluated by *outcomes* rather than by adherence to processes or artifacts? What does that imply about "we followed the methodology exactly" as a defense of a failed project?
2. A regulatory change invalidates half your assumptions mid-project. Trace the domains affected — at least four — and explain how each responds.
3. Distinguish leading from lagging indicators, and explain why a measurement plan built entirely on lagging indicators is like driving by the rearview mirror.
