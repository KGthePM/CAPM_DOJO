# LEC-03 — Tailoring: The Art of the Well-Fitted Approach

*CAPM Training Dojo · Professor's Lecture Series · Session Three*

Mr. Gomez. You have learned the principles and surveyed the domains. Today you learn what PMI expects you to *do* with them: **tailoring**, the deliberate adaptation of the project management approach, governance, and processes to make them more suitable for the environment and the work at hand.

Here is the cold truth. Most methodologies — including, explicitly, this Guide — *tell you not to apply them verbatim*. They are menus, not mandates. The inexperienced practitioner wears the framework off the rack, unchanged, adding cost and ceremony without value. The professional tailors. Off the rack never fits properly, and PMI has built an exam section on that fact.

## Why Tailor? (§3.2)

Tailoring is performed to better suit the **organization**, the **operating environment**, and the **project needs**. The variables are familiar: criticality, stakeholder count, risk appetite, culture. The rigor demanded by a nuclear reactor dwarfs that of an office building; the communication plan for ten people collapses at two hundred. PMI tests both edges of the blade: *too few* processes omit key activities, while *more processes than required* is costly and wasteful. Tailoring is calibration, not decoration.

The benefits are concrete: more commitment from team members who helped tailor the approach, a customer-oriented focus, and more efficient use of resources. The alternative — an **unmodified framework** applied without regard to context — is always the wrong answer dressed in confident clothing.

- *Bar Exam:* know the two failure modes — process starvation and process bloat — and the three named benefits. Also the drivers: size, duration, complexity, industry, organizational culture, project management maturity.

## What to Tailor (§3.3)

Five project aspects are tailorable. Learn the list cold; the exam offers distractors like "tailoring the strategic plan" or "tailoring the principles."

**Life cycle and development approach selection.** Deciding on the life cycle and its phases is itself tailoring. Some large projects run combinations *simultaneously* — the classic PMBOK example is a new data center: predictive for construction, iterative for the computing capabilities. Viewed from the project level this is a **hybrid approach**, even though each sub-team experiences only its own approach.

**Processes.** Five verbs, verbatim. Process elements may be **added** (rigor or coverage — independent inspections on safety-critical work), **modified** (to suit the team — reformatting documents for members with vision limitations), **removed** (no longer earning their cost — dropping formal minutes for a colocated team), **blended** (mixing elements for extra value — folding appreciative inquiry into lessons-learned meetings), and **aligned** (harmonizing definitions across disciplines so "risk management" means the same thing to engineers and contractors).

**Engagement.** Three sub-considerations: **people** (matching skills to project type — a time-constrained project wants experienced hands), **empowerment** (how much local decision making is deferred to the team versus supervised), and **integration** (forging one team from internal staff, contractors, and other external contributors).

**Tools.** Selecting software and equipment is tailoring. The team usually has the best insight into suitable tools, but choices may be tempered by cost and constrained by organizational leaders.

**Methods and artifacts.** The *means* used to achieve outcomes should suit the environment and culture; documents, templates, and artifacts should suit both project and organization. Section 4 of the Guide exists to feed this step.

- *Bar Exam:* the five process verbs (add, modify, remove, blend, align) and the five tailorable aspects are prime matching-question fodder. The data-center hybrid example appears nearly verbatim on exams.

## The Tailoring Process (§3.4)

Tailoring is a *sequence*, not a shrug. Before it begins, the project environment must be analyzed and understood. Then four steps:

**1. Select the initial development approach.** The team applies knowledge of the product, the delivery cadence, and the available options. The marquee tool is the **suitability filter** — an informational instrument that evaluates culture, project team, and project factors and generates a diagnostic visual suggesting whether the project leans predictive, hybrid, or adaptive. It does not decide; it *informs* the decision.

**2. Tailor for the organization.** Teams own and improve their processes, but organizations require approval and oversight, and many mandate a methodology as the starting point for repeatability and consistent capability measurement. Tailoring must align with governance and policy, and teams may need to *justify* a tailored approach to show it threatens no larger strategic or stewardship goals. Two constraints bite hardest: large safety-critical projects (extra oversight) and projects performed under contract (contract terms may dictate the life cycle or methodology outright). Approval logic scales with blast radius: tailoring that affects only the team — internal meetings, who works where — may be approved by the project manager; tailoring that affects external groups needs the **PMO** or, in adaptive-leaning organizations, the **value delivery office (VDO)**. Mark the distinction: a PMO reviews and approves; a VDO *enables* — coaching teams, building adaptive capability, and mentoring sponsors and product owners rather than supervising them.

**3. Tailor for the project.** Three attribute families, each interrogated with questions. *Product/deliverable:* compliance and criticality; product type (a building is easy to describe; software is not); market and regulation; technology stability; time frame; requirements stability; security; and whether incremental delivery with feedback is feasible. *Project team:* size, geography, distribution, experience, and access to the customer. *Culture:* buy-in, trust, empowerment, and alignment of organizational values.

**4. Implement ongoing improvement.** Tailoring is not a one-time exercise. Review points, **phase gates**, and retrospectives all offer opportunities to inspect and adapt the process, development approach, and delivery frequency. Keeping the team engaged in improving its process fosters pride of ownership, demonstrates trust and empowerment, and reflects a mindset of innovation. Indeed, how an organization tailors can itself be tailored — but most use some or all of these four steps.

- *Bar Exam:* the four-step order is directly testable ("What comes first?" — selecting the initial development approach). Know the suitability filter's role (informational, not decisive), the PMO-versus-VDO distinction, and the two hard constraints (safety-critical oversight, contractually mandated approaches).

## Tailoring the Performance Domains (§3.5)

Each of the eight domains from LEC-02 can itself be tailored — the *principles guiding practitioner behavior* as it happens. The flavor of each domain's considerations:

**Stakeholders.** Collaborative environment or not; internal, external, or both; which communication technologies are cost-effective; language and cultural diversity; how many stakeholders; and their relationships — the more networks a stakeholder joins, the more complex the webs of information *and misinformation* they receive.

**Project team.** Colocation and time zones; diverse viewpoints; how members are identified, full- or part-time, contractor availability; team culture and how it shapes — and is shaped by — tailoring; team development; special needs and training.

**Development approach and life cycle.** Which approach suits the product — if adaptive, incremental or iterative, or hybrid; what phases the life cycle contains; what audit and governance policies exist.

**Planning.** How environmental factors influence deliverables; what drives durations; cost-estimating and budgeting policies, including under adaptive approaches; single versus multiple procurements; and whether local procurement law integrates with organizational policy, affecting contract auditing.

**Project work.** Which management processes fit the culture and complexity; how knowledge is managed to foster collaboration; what information is collected, how, and with what technology; whether lessons learned reach future projects through an accessible repository.

**Delivery.** Requirements management systems; validation and control policies; quality policies, tools, and templates; industry standards and regulatory constraints; handling *unstable requirements*; and sustainability.

**Uncertainty.** Risk appetite and tolerance; how threats and opportunities are identified within the chosen approach; how complexity, technological uncertainty, novelty, cadence, and progress tracking affect the project; whether project size demands detailed or simplified risk management; and strategic importance — breakthrough aims raise risk.

**Measurement.** How value is measured, financial *and* nonfinancial; how the project enables reporting on benefits realization during *and after* the project; and status reporting requirements.

- *Bar Exam:* expect scenario questions mapping a symptom to a domain's tailoring response — unstable requirements point to Delivery; benefits-realization reporting to Measurement. Notice how domain tailoring echoes the principles.

## Diagnostics: Did the Tailoring Work? (§3.6)

How do you know whether your tailoring succeeded? **Periodic reviews — retrospectives and lessons-learned sessions — are the primary diagnostic instruments.** Teams without retrospectives can read the gauges instead: issues, threats, quality assurance statistics, and stakeholder feedback. The Guide's Table 3-1 pairs common situations with tailoring responses, and the pairings are exam gold: poor-quality deliverables call for added feedback loops, verification, and quality assurance; team members unsure how to proceed call for added guidance and training; long approval delays suggest reassigning decisions closer to the work so people are empowered to decide rapidly; too much work in progress or a high rate of scrap suggests value stream mapping and kanban boards to make the work visible; disengaged stakeholders call for genuine *engagement* rather than simple *communication*; poor visibility into progress suggests ensuring metrics are captured, analyzed, and displayed in team and stakeholder meetings; and recurring issues the team merely reacts to suggest exploring root causes for gaps in project processes. Underneath every row: diagnose the symptom, adjust the process, involve the people.

## Key Terms

- **Tailoring** — deliberate adaptation of the approach, governance, and processes to suit the environment and work at hand
- **Suitability filter** — informational tool evaluating culture, team, and project factors to generate a diagnostic visual suggesting predictive, hybrid, or adaptive
- **Tailoring process** — select initial development approach, tailor for the organization, tailor for the project, implement ongoing improvement
- **Add, modify, remove, blend, align** — the five ways process elements are tailored
- **Hybrid approach** — deliberate combination of development approaches, possibly run simultaneously within one project
- **VDO (value delivery office)** — enabling body that coaches teams and mentors sponsors/product owners in adaptive organizations, rather than overseeing them
- **Ongoing improvement** — continuous inspection and adaptation of the tailoring via review points, phase gates, and retrospectives
- **Diagnostics** — retrospectives, issues, QA statistics, and stakeholder feedback used to evaluate whether tailoring is working
