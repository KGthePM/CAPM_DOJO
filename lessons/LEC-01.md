# LEC-01 — Foundations: Projects, Value Delivery & the 12 Principles

*CAPM Training Dojo · Professor's Lecture Series · Session One*

Sit down, Mr. Gomez. Punctuality noted and appreciated — do keep it up.

You arrive here with a Google certificate and, I am told, genuine enthusiasm. Splendid. Enthusiasm is a fine appetizer, but the CAPM exam is the main course, and PMI grades with the warmth of a February nor'easter. So we begin properly: with **foundations**. Not the flashy stuff — the load-bearing stuff. Miss today's material and everything that follows collapses like a cheap tent.

## What Makes a Project a Project

Let us start with the definition you will recite in your sleep by December: a **project** is a *temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result*.

Two words do all the work. **Temporary** — it has a defined beginning and end, unlike your aunt's gossip or *operations*, the ongoing repetitive work that keeps the lights on. **Unique** — the deliverable is distinguishable from anything else; nobody commissions a project to build the identical bridge twice. Now, Mr. Gomez, a favorite exam trick: "routine maintenance," "recurring monthly reporting," "answering service tickets" — operations, all of them. But "migrate the ticketing system to a new platform" — that, dear boy, is a project. Temporary, unique, done.

And here is the philosophical turn PMI took in the 7th Edition that separates it from your Google coursework: projects do not exist to produce *outputs*. Outputs are merely the **deliverables**. Deliverables drive **outcomes** — the change they cause in the world — and outcomes yield **benefits**, and benefits yield **value**, something of worth, importance, or usefulness. Memorize that causal chain: **outputs → outcomes → benefits → value**. PMI tests it, and worse, PMI *believes* it.

## From Process to Principles: The Great Reformation

For decades the PMBOK Guide was a **process-based standard**: dozens of processes, each with inputs, tools, techniques, and outputs — the famous ITTOs you may have heard veterans mutter about. Effective, in its way. Also prescriptive. Rigid as a New England stone wall.

So PMI tore up the blueprint. The 7th Edition is a **principles-based standard**. Understand why, because "why" is where exam questions live: project management began evolving faster than a process catalog could be reprinted, and the practice now spans the full delivery spectrum — **predictive to adaptive and everything in between**. A prescriptive rulebook cannot cover that territory. Principles can.

And mark this well: **the principles are NOT prescriptive**. They do not command; they *guide the behavior of people involved in projects*. They are internally consistent — no principle contradicts another — though they may overlap. They also harmonize with the **PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct**, whose four values are **Responsibility, Respect, Fairness, and Honesty**. Complementary, not duplicative — a distinction PMI finds delicious.

## The Value Delivery System

A project is not an orphan. It lives inside a **system for value delivery**: an organization's portfolio of strategic investments, its programs, its projects, and its **operations**, all wired together and governed by an organizational **governance system** that provides oversight, control, and decision-making structure. Strategy flows down from senior leadership through portfolios to programs and projects; deliverables flow out to operations; and information — this is the elegant part — flows *both directions*. Operations report back fixes and adjustments; projects report progress toward outcomes and value back up the chain.

The exam-worthy insight: focusing on value delivery changes the perspective from merely *governing* projects to attending to the **value chain that links projects to organizational strategy**. A project that delivers its deliverables on time but produces no benefit is, in the 7th Edition's eyes, a beautifully executed failure. Harsh? Perhaps. Correct? Unquestionably.

## The Twelve Principles

Now the heart of the lecture. Twelve principles, no prescribed order, no weighting — PMI lists them as equals, like a good dean treats faculty. Learn each label verbatim; the exam matches *language*, not vibes.

**1. Be a Diligent, Respectful, and Caring Steward.** You are entrusted with resources, people, and trust itself — inside the organization (finances, fair treatment of team members, wise use of authority) and outside it (environmental sustainability, community impact, advancing the profession). Stewardship runs on four duties: **integrity, care, trustworthiness, and compliance**. The professorial gloss: leave the place better than you found it, and don't pocket the silverware.
- *Bar Exam:* watch for scenario questions about conflicting guidelines or misuse of resources — the answer is the stewardly one: seek counsel, be transparent, act ethically.

**2. Create a Collaborative Project Team Environment.** Teams that collaborate outperform collections of individuals grinding in parallel. Collaboration is built through **team agreements** (shared behavioral norms), supportive **organizational structures**, and processes that make individual effort *synergistic*. The whole exceeds the sum of its parts — or the parts have failed.
- *Bar Exam:* if a team shares ownership of objectives and produces jointly, that's this principle, not "team building picnic."

**3. Effectively Engage with Stakeholders.** Stakeholders — individuals, groups, or organizations who may affect, be affected by, or *perceive themselves to be affected by* the project — come and go, and their influence waxes and wanes across the life cycle. Engage them proactively, from start to finish, through **two-way communication** built on solid relationships. Engagement goes deeper than communication: it assimilates other perspectives and shapes shared solutions.
- *Bar Exam:* the key words are *proactive* and *early*. A team that identifies stakeholders during initiation, not after the angry phone call.

**4. Focus on Value.** Value is the north star. Outcomes, benefits, value — not just deliverables. The team keeps the *long-range* performance in view and makes choices that preserve and enhance value, balancing short-term cost against long-term worth like a careful Yankee balancing a checkbook.
- *Bar Exam:* questions where the "right" answer prioritizes long-term outcomes over churning out features.

**5. Recognize, Evaluate, and Respond to System Interactions.** A project *is* a system — interacting, interdependent components functioning as a whole — and it sits within larger systems (programs, portfolios, organizations): a **system of systems**. A change in one component ripples. Teams take a **holistic view**, balancing "inside-out" and "outside-in" perspectives.
- *Bar Exam:* the distractor answers are *reactive* or *isolated*; the correct one anticipates ripple effects across the whole.

**6. Demonstrate Leadership Behaviors.** Authority is granted by the org chart; **leadership is earned and demonstrated** — and anyone on the team can exercise it, not just the person with the title. No single style wins universally: directive in chaos, delegative with skilled staff, facilitative among squabbling executives. Effective leadership *fits the situation*, and — good news for you — it can be learned.
- *Bar Exam:* the answer that says leadership is situational and not confined to a role.

**7. Tailor Based on Context.** No two projects are identical, so no single approach fits all. Tailoring is the *deliberate adaptation* of the approach, governance, and processes to suit the project's unique characteristics and environment — within the boundaries the organization authorizes. An untailored process adds cost and schedule without adding value. Like a good suit: off the rack will never fit properly.
- *Bar Exam:* "the PMO's methodology must be followed exactly" is always the wrong answer; "adapt within organizational policy" is the right one.

**8. Build Quality Into Processes and Deliverables.** Quality is not an inspection at the end — that merely finds the bodies. Quality is *built in*: deliverables fit for purpose per acceptance criteria, meeting stakeholder expectations, minimal defects, less rework. Turn the crank correctly and the output is correct.
- *Bar Exam:* prevention over inspection. Every time. Write it on your hand if you must.

**9. Navigate Complexity.** Complexity arises from human behavior, system behavior, ambiguity, uncertainty, and innovation — and it is *emergent*, appearing where interactions combine unpredictably. The team proactively senses, assesses, and adapts. Do not fight the fog; learn to sail in it.
- *Bar Exam:* complexity comes from *interactions and emergence*, not merely "many tasks" — that's just a big project, which is different.

**10. Optimize Risk Responses.** A risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, affects objectives — *positively or negatively*. Threats and opportunities both. Teams continuously identify, evaluate, and respond to known and emergent risks, **maximizing opportunities and reducing exposure to threats**. Yes, Mr. Gomez, upside risk exists; the exam adores candidates who forget it.
- *Bar Exam:* the answer that treats risk as ongoing and two-sided, not a one-time negative-event list.

**11. Embrace Adaptability and Resiliency.** Projects rarely perform exactly as planned — new requirements, shifting stakeholders, the world being the world. **Adaptability** is responding to changing conditions; **resiliency** is both *absorbing* impacts and *recovering quickly* from setbacks. Clinging to early plans after circumstances change is not fidelity; it is folly.
- *Bar Exam:* distinguish the two terms precisely — respond vs. absorb-and-recover. They will try to swap them on you.

**12. Enable Change to Achieve the Envisioned Future State.** Projects exist to move an organization from its current state to a future state — and people, bless them, resist. Enabling change requires structures, strategies, and *stakeholder engagement and motivation*. Too much change too fast breeds change fatigue; too little leaves value unrealized.
- *Bar Exam:* the answer that pairs the technical transition with people-focused adoption. Deliverables do not adopt themselves.

## Key Terms

- **Project** — temporary endeavor creating a unique product, service, or result
- **Operations** — ongoing, repetitive organizational work (not a project)
- **Outputs → Outcomes → Benefits → Value** — the deliverable-to-value causal chain
- **Value delivery system** — portfolios, programs, projects, and operations linked by information flow, guided by governance
- **Principles-based standard** — non-prescriptive guidance for behavior across all delivery approaches
- **Stewardship** — diligent, respectful, caring trusteeship (integrity, care, trustworthiness, compliance)
- **System of systems** — projects nested within programs, portfolios, and organizations
- **Tailoring** — deliberate adaptation of approach, governance, and processes to context

## Addendum — Portfolio, Program, Project: The Hierarchy

A correction from the professor's red pen, Mr. Gomez: earlier we named this hierarchy in passing; the exam demands you *command* it. Three tiers, three organizing logics — and the distractors are always the adjacent tier.

- **Portfolio** — projects, programs, subsidiary portfolios, and *operations* managed as a group to achieve the organization's **strategic objectives**. The organizing logic is *strategy*: a portfolio may contain utterly unrelated work, united only by its claim on the organization's money and attention.
- **Program** — *related* projects, subsidiary programs, and program activities managed in a **coordinated way to obtain benefits not available from managing them individually**. The organizing logic is *shared benefit through coordination*: shared infrastructure, integrated schedules, one voice to stakeholders.
- **Project** — our old friend: a temporary endeavor producing a unique product, service, or result.

Work the example until it is reflexive: the *digital transformation portfolio* contains the *new CRM capability program*, which contains the *legacy data migration project*. If a question says "related projects, benefits unavailable individually," the answer is **program** — every time. If it says "grouped to achieve strategic objectives," the answer is **portfolio**. And mark this: operations live *inside* portfolios as ongoing concerns, which is precisely how the whole thing stays wired into the value delivery system from Session One.
